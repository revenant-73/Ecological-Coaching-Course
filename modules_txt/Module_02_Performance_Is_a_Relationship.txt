MODULE 2: Performance Is a Relationship
Description: Core concepts: constraints, affordances, perception–action coupling.
==========================================================================================================================

By now, you may already feel the tension.

If you step in less…
if you talk less…
if you stop fixing things immediately…

What actually shapes performance?

The answer is simpler—and more demanding—than most coaching models suggest.

### Performance Is Not a Trait

It’s a Relationship.

Every volleyball action emerges from the interaction of three things:

- **The Player** (ability, confidence, fatigue)
- **The Environment** (space, noise, opposition)
- **The Task** (rules, scoring, contacts, structure)

Change one—and behavior changes.

Most coaching effort is poured into the player: their technique, effort, or focus.

But that’s the smallest and least reliable lever you have—especially when the game speeds up.

### Volleyball Is an Open Skill Sport

That’s coach-speak for one thing:
The game refuses to behave.

The ball doesn’t arrive on schedule.
Opponents don’t cooperate.
Teammates make unscripted decisions.

And yet we often train volleyball as if it should behave.
As if cleaning it up will make it stable.

Then we act surprised when it collapses under pressure.

The chaos isn’t the problem.
Our attempt to eliminate it is.

### A Shift in Responsibility

Ecological coaching doesn’t ask you to coach less.
It asks you to coach earlier—at the level where behavior is actually shaped.

Instead of asking:
“What should I say to the player?”

You begin asking:
“What is this task making inevitable?”

### Clearing Up a Few Misconceptions

**“Ecological coaching is chaos.”**
No. It’s intentional instability—designed to clarify perception and decision-making.

**“No feedback means no improvement.”**
No. Feedback still matters.
But adaptation accelerates when players are supported in discovering solutions, not just executing instructions.

**“This is anti-technique.”**
No. Technique still matters.
But here, form follows function—shaped by constraints, context, and intention, not isolation.

### A Bridge Forward

From here on, the question changes.

We spend less time asking:
“What should I say?”

And far more time asking:
“What is this task teaching?”

### REFLECTION: On-Court Challenge — Module 2
Questions/Prompts:
1. Prompt 1: The Task Change
   Instruction: In your next practice, change one task constraint (rule, scoring, space, or number of contacts).
   Context: Describe the activity and the single constraint you changed. Be specific.
2. Prompt 2: Your Prediction
   Instruction: Before running the activity, predict what behavior you expect to change.
   Context: What did you think players would do differently because of this task change? Focus on behavior, not technique.
3. Prompt 3: What Emerged
   Instruction: During the activity, avoid giving instructions about how to perform the skill. Observe what happens.
   Context: What behaviors actually changed once the task started? What adjustments did players make without being told?
4. Prompt 4: The Surprise
   Instruction: After the activity, reflect on what you didn’t anticipate.
   Context: What surprised you? This could be a solution you didn’t expect, a problem that disappeared, or a new challenge that emerged.

Closing Instruction: Reflection Reminder: Focus on what the task taught, not what you said.
