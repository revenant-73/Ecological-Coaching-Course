MODULE 2: Performance Is a Relationship
Why behavior changes without saying a word

From Control to Conditions

If Module 1 asked you to notice why you intervene,
Module 2 asks you to notice where performance actually comes from.

Because if performance were simply a trait inside the athlete—
a matter of effort, toughness, or technique—

then instruction would be enough.

Say the right thing.
Fix the right detail.
Repeat it until it sticks.

But volleyball doesn’t work that way.

Not consistently.
Not under pressure.
Not when the game speeds up.


Performance Is Not a Trait

It’s a relationship.

Every action on the court emerges from the interaction of three things:

The Player
(skill, confidence, fatigue, history)

The Environment
(space, noise, score, opponents, consequences)

The Task
(rules, scoring, constraints, structure)

Change one—and behavior changes.

You already know this.

You see it when the same player:
swings differently late in a set
passes differently at 23–23
makes better decisions when consequences feel real

Same player.
Different relationship.


Why Player-Focused Fixes Have Limits

Most coaching effort still starts with the player:
technique cues
reminders
corrections
motivation

Sometimes that works—and it still has a place.

But in complex, fast, open-skill games, it has limits.

When the game speeds up:
attention narrows
verbal information drops out
habits take over

If the environment and the task don’t support the behavior you want,
player-focused fixes carry the entire load.

That’s a heavy lift under pressure.

Ecological coaching doesn’t remove instruction.
It reduces how much instruction has to do when it matters most.


Volleyball Still Refuses to Behave

Volleyball is an open skill sport.

That means:
information arrives late
decisions are time-pressured
movement is rarely repeatable

And yet we often train it as if stability were the goal.

Clean reps.
Predictable tosses.
Isolated technique.

Then we’re surprised when those skills don’t survive the mess.

The chaos isn’t a flaw in the athlete.
It’s the defining feature of the game.


A Shift in Coaching Responsibility

Ecological coaching doesn’t ask you to coach less.

It asks you to coach earlier—
at the level where behavior is shaped before words are needed.

Instead of asking:
What should I tell the player?

You begin asking:
What is this task inviting—or forcing—the player to do?

Every task:
makes some actions easier
makes others harder
teaches something, whether you intend it or not

You’re always coaching.

The question is whether the environment is helping you—or quietly undoing you.


What This Does (And Does Not) Mean

This does not mean:
you stop correcting
you abandon technique
you let confusion linger indefinitely

It means:
instruction becomes more precise
intervention becomes more intentional
learning survives pressure more often

Care still matters.
Connection still matters.

You’re just choosing a lever that holds when the game doesn’t cooperate.


REFLECTION: ON-COURT CHALLENGE — MODULE 2

This is not about getting it right.
It’s about seeing differently.

Prompt 1: The Task Change
In your next practice, change one task constraint
(rule, scoring, space, or number of contacts).

Describe the activity and the single change you made. Be specific.

Prompt 2: Your Prediction
Before starting, predict what behavior you expect to change.
Focus on decisions or movement, not technique.

Prompt 3: What Emerged
During the activity, avoid giving instructions about how to perform the skill.
Observe what actually changes once the task is live.

Prompt 4: The Surprise
Afterward, reflect on what you didn’t anticipate.
A solution you didn’t expect.
A problem that disappeared.
A new challenge that emerged.

Closing Instruction:
Focus on what the task taught—not what you said.


Bridge Forward

How do you translate what you’re seeing into a change the game can feel—
without stopping it?
